This document is a starting point for helping you design more inclusive
educational materials, with links to useful online resources.
Inclusivity is about more than just being able to tick things off a checklist, and
involves thinking about:
pedagogy (e.g. teaching with movement-based or game-based activities
to engage more learners),
different ways of learni ng (e.g. providing ways to engage with the activity
solo or in a group, visually or aurally),
whether the topics and voices featured in the content are reflecting
the full picture (e.g., when teaching about the British Empire, whose
perspectives are being highlighted?)
This document focuses more on surface-level features
like fonts and images used, because they are “low-
hanging fruit” – easier to correct. These things are
nevertheless important to get right. But once you’ve got
them right, it doesn’t mean the job is done! It is still
extremely important to think about the deeper features
mentioned above. The end of this document will offer
some pointers on how to get started with those.

Using diverse names
Representation matters. As anyone with an unusual name will know, it can feel
othering when names like yours never come up in the example questions or
stories in educational materials. This can feel even worse when in fact you’re in
a setting where “unusual” names are the norm, and yet only names from the
historically dominant tradition appear in all your learning materials – it sends a
message that people like you don’t belong in that space. So use a diverse
selection of names whenever you create example questions, stories, or
characters.

Images
The same goes for images as for names. When including example images of
people in slides, worksheets, and other educational materials, ensure that they
represent a diverse range of skin tones, hair colours, body types, and
disabilities.
WATCH OUT FOR... WATCH OUT FOR... WATCH OUT FOR... AI-GENERATED IMAGES AI-GENERATED IMAGES AI-GENERATED IMAGES
WATCH OUT FOR... AI-GENERATED IMAGES
AI-generated images, if you have not prompted them carefully, are by default likely to
favour images of men over women, white people over people of colour, thin people
over fat people, etc. They will usually reproduce harmful stereotypes. E.g., if you
ask for an image of a doctor, it will generate a white man, and if you ask for a nurse, a
white woman, both usually young, slim and conventionally attractive. There are a host
of other issues with using AI-generated images, such as copyright infringement of
scraped training data, environmental footprint of hardware , exploitative labour
practices , and the political agendas of big tech companies .
